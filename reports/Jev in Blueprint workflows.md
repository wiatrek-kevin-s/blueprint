# Put Jev in the harness, not the planner

Blueprint should adopt Jev in a narrow, staged way, and never inside the shipped planner. **The first and only near-term step is to try Jev as a second-opinion grader in the repo-only eval harness.** That harness is not shipped, already pays for a model, runs only invented prompts, and relies on regex heuristics its own README calls fragile. **Defer** an opt-in, vendor-neutral "typed decision check" in the kits Blueprint hands off. If it comes later, it should be a pre-filter in the coordinator's verification step and never the thing that marks work done or allows a risky action. **For users' products**, add short vendor-neutral guidance about typed decision models to the existing "AI inside the product" rules, and let run-time research surface Jev or an alternative with an "as of" date. Blueprint must not store a recommendation for Jev itself. **Avoid** calling Jev from the planner's own decisions (route, size, one-way doors, audit severity). Those decisions happen a few times per run, and the host model already holds the context. Sending the person's project to a third-party API also conflicts with Rules 6, 9 and 10. The evidence behind this caution is solid. Independent tests confirm that Jev is fast (about 5x faster than cheap LLMs, not the claimed 20–200x) and very cheap against frontier models. They also show that its calibration varies from task to task, that it can be steered by injected text in the state, and that it is a two-month-old early-access product with no published SLA. Much of the web evidence below comes from search snippets because the session's network blocked most pages. Figures marked *(snippet)* are unverified.

## Jev is fast and cheap, but its calibration is uneven and it can be steered

Jev is TypeSafe AI's non-generative "System One" model. It takes a `state` (text or JSON) and a set of typed questions (`choice`, `score`, `noul` for yes/no) and returns a probability distribution over each question's allowed answers in one pass. It never returns prose ([Flavio Copes](https://flaviocopes.com/jev/); [TypeSafe quickstart](https://docs.typesafe.ai/introduction/quickstart), *snippet*). It launched in early access on **15 September 2026** ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/09/16/typesafe-ai-debuts-model-for-machines-that-plays-doom/5296711), *snippet*). TypeSafe's help centre lists a price of **$0.042 per million input tokens, with output free** ([help.typesafe.ai](https://help.typesafe.ai/articles/5187202169-how-much-does-jev-cost), *snippet*), and several sources call that early-access pricing that is "subject to change" ([developersdigest](https://www.developersdigest.tech/blog/typesafe-jev-system-one-models-release-guide-2026), *snippet*). Signups were paused on 22 September because of demand and reopened on 27 September without the $5 free credit ([@typesafeai](https://x.com/typesafeai/status/2104337822350221795)). Basic facts are still unsettled. The context window is reported as **32K by Vercel and 262,144 by OpenRouter** ([OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/typesafe/jev-1.13), *snippet*). Rate-limit figures conflict across unofficial sources, and the exact response JSON schema could not be confirmed because docs.typesafe.ai was unreachable.

The two independent benchmarks read in full give a consistent picture. On 500 spam and 500 Banking77 messages, PavelRavvich found Jev statistically tied with a cheap LLM on spam and **3.4 points worse on Banking77**, with the worst calibration error (ECE) on the shared subset. Its speed advantage was about **5–6x at the median**, and its cost ranged from parity to only 2.2x cheaper than the cheap LLM, though 84–139x cheaper than a frontier model ([PavelRavvich/jev-bench](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PavelRavvich/jev-bench/main/article/jev-article.md)). nibzard ran 40,226 decisions. To keep error at or below 5% on Banking77, the confidence threshold had to be 0.96, which left only **51.2% of cases** answered automatically. A threshold chosen on validation data missed its target on CLINC150 test data, and no threshold met the target on multi-label NLU++. When every offered option was wrong, Jev's mean confidence was still **53.7%**, against 5.8–22% for the LLMs ([nibzard](https://github.com/nibzard/decision-model-benchmark)). Vals AI reports that the same model had the best calibration of 12 systems on claim verification and the worst on LegalBench ([Vals AI](https://www.vals.ai/blogs/independent-evaluation-of-jev), *snippet*). A radiology preprint found raw probabilities poorly calibrated until isotonic recalibration was applied ([arXiv 2609.27607](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.27607), *snippet*).

The security finding matters most for Blueprint. Datadog Security Labs placed a "counterfeit policy" in the content Jev was judging, and **Jev's prompt-injection recall fell from 82.90% to 3.88%**. In LangChain-style tool-risk gating, a counterfeit policy planted in a user-editable profile field cut the mean `is_risky` score from 0.893 to 0.290, below the 0.5 block threshold ([Datadog Security Labs](https://securitylabs.datadoghq.com/articles/jev-as-a-judge-how-counterfeit-policy-steers-jev/)). Reviewers agree on one rule. Jev's answer is a signal for deterministic code to act on. It is never the only boundary. Reviewers also advise pinning `jev-1.13.0` rather than the moving `jev-latest` alias, and escalating low-confidence cases to an LLM or a person ([wavect](https://wavect.io/blog/jev-ai-decision-model-review/), *snippet*; [aibuilderclub](https://www.aibuilderclub.com/blog/jev-engineering-guide), *snippet*). On the business side, TypeSafe offers zero data retention only to enterprise customers, hosts only in the US West Coast, reportedly signs no HIPAA BAA, and publishes no SLA ([befailproof.ai](https://befailproof.ai/jev/privacy-data-retention/), *snippet*; [vendortrustindex](https://vendortrustindex.com/vendors/jev), *snippet*). Two further reports could not be confirmed from primary sources: a reported $870M Series A ([Tech Times](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/328875/20261013/typesafe-ai-raises-870m-jev-decision-model-that-never-generates-text.htm)), and a single-source claim that TypeSafe holds no enterprise security certifications. Treat both as unconfirmed.

## The planner's decisions fit Jev's input format, but its rules and economics rule it out

Reading the repository shows about 30 typed decisions that could be posed to a model like Jev. They include route selection as a five-way enum with an "ask if nothing fits clearly" rule ([skill/SKILL.md:46-55](../skill/SKILL.md)), size as small, medium or large with a floor of medium for personal data ([skill/SKILL.md:77](../skill/SKILL.md)), one-way versus two-way doors ([skill/references/02-landscape.md:29](../skill/references/02-landscape.md)), audit severity and likelihood on 1–10 scales with labels ([skill/references/route-audit.md:82-90](../skill/references/route-audit.md)), Amend's stuck-build triage and change classification ([skill/references/08-change-control.md:13-27](../skill/references/08-change-control.md)), and the final review's explicit threshold ("40 or more, or severity 9+ with likelihood 3+ → Fix now", [skill/templates/kit-04-final-review.md:27-35](../skill/templates/kit-04-final-review.md)). Several of these rubrics already read as if written for a classifier. A model returning calibrated probabilities would also map neatly onto Blueprint's "70% rule" for two-way doors ([skill/SKILL.md:115](../skill/SKILL.md)) and its high/medium/low assumption confidence ([skill/templates/state.md:50](../skill/templates/state.md)).

Calling Jev from the shipped planner is still the wrong move, for four reasons. **First, the rules block it.** Blueprint is Markdown run by the host Claude, so any Jev call would be a tool call that sends the person's interview or code to a third party. Rule 10 and the read-only rule say to ask before "anything that touches … live services or the network on the project's behalf". The only exemption is looking up *public* data by name ([skill/SKILL.md:24](../skill/SKILL.md); [skill/references/existing-projects.md:25](../skill/references/existing-projects.md)). Rule 9 forbids asking for keys in chat ([skill/SKILL.md:23](../skill/SKILL.md)), and lightning mode can never assume keys or sign-ups ([skill/references/lightning.md:32](../skill/references/lightning.md)). The repository does not say whether Claude-app code execution can reach the network at all. **Second, the economics fail.** Each of these decisions happens once or a few times per run, while the host model already holds the full context. Jev's per-call advantage over a model that is already reasoning in the same turn is close to zero, which matches the general break-even rule that one-off decisions inside a turn are not worth an extra call ([OpenRouter: Jev vs LLM](https://openrouter.ai/blog/tutorials/jev-vs-llm-when-to-use-each/), *snippet*). **Third, Blueprint's culture of evidence works against opaque answers.** "Nothing is done without evidence" ([skill/SKILL.md:26](../skill/SKILL.md)), and "A finding without evidence is not reported" ([skill/references/route-audit.md:83](../skill/references/route-audit.md)). The audit's "Fix now" re-check explicitly asks a fresh subagent to "show the evidence" ([skill/references/route-audit.md:88](../skill/references/route-audit.md)), which a typed probability cannot do. **Fourth, the stakes are lopsided.** If a door is wrongly classed as two-way, the person is never asked, and that is the failure Blueprint exists to prevent. Jev's documented tendency to stay confident when every option is wrong makes this worse. The deterministic checks `check_kit.py` and `check_tasks.py` must also stay standard-library-only, so they can be walked "by hand" when code cannot run ([skill/scripts/check_kit.py:55](../skill/scripts/check_kit.py); [README.md:75](../README.md)). A Jev-backed check would break both properties.

One planner idea deserves to be parked rather than rejected: scoring file relevance in Audit across very large repositories, the pattern community tools like Jevgrep use. Its evidence is weak. The claimed 28.6% saving comes from 10 tasks and excludes Jev's own charges ([RuntimeWire](https://runtimewire.com/article/david-zhang-jevgrep-coding-agent-costs), *snippet*). It would also send the person's code to TypeSafe, so it would need the same ask-first treatment as any other network action.

## Start in the eval harness, where Jev grades transcripts and calls in a person when unsure

The eval harness is the only place where no rule blocks Jev and where the decisions repeat. It is repo-only, so "nothing here ships in the skill zip" ([evals/README.md:3](../evals/README.md)). Its prompts and fixtures are invented ([evals/README.md:16-18](../evals/README.md)), so no private data leaves. It already spends roughly 100–280 USD per release on a paid model ([evals/README.md:168](../evals/README.md)). Its scoring rests on regexes that are, in effect, typed questions over transcripts. The route is parsed by `ROUTE_RE` ([evals/run_routing.py:91](../evals/run_routing.py)). Whether lightning mode asked anything is judged by "two or more question marks", which the README calls "a heuristic on purpose" ([evals/run_routing.py:246-252](../evals/run_routing.py); [evals/README.md:111](../evals/README.md)). Whether a final message asks anything more is a "?" in the last 600 characters or an approve/confirm word ([evals/run_behaviour.py:417-419](../evals/run_behaviour.py)). Several outcomes are scored only by reading: whether a `disagrees` kit kept the person's choice, whether `goes-quiet` recorded guesses as answers, and whether an extra audit finding is false ([evals/README.md:145-146](../evals/README.md), [evals/README.md:194](../evals/README.md)).

The proposed design is a small grader module, for example `evals/jev_grade.py`, that calls TypeSafe's HTTP endpoint through `urllib` so the harness stays "bash and Python 3 only". It sits behind an adapter, because the response schema is still unverified. It asks one batched request per transcript: a `choice` over {new, audit, redesign, operate, amend, none} for route, `noul` for "contains a question round", `noul` for "final message asks the person something", `noul` for "kit kept the person's overridden choice", and `noul` for "a guess was recorded as the person's answer". The model is pinned to `jev-1.13.0`, and the key is read from an environment variable that is never committed. The regex result **stays the score of record**. Jev only flags a transcript for human reading when it disagrees with the regex or when |p − 0.5| is small. That turns the README's existing "read the transcript when one fails" practice into a targeted queue. This matches the cascade-and-abstain pattern the literature supports: a preprint found that keeping the half of Jev's decisions with the largest |p − 0.5| raised median accuracy from 0.793 to 0.933 ([arXiv 2609.29429](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.29429), *snippet*). The pass marks (`PASS_OVERALL = 0.90`, `PASS_ROUTE = 0.85`, [evals/run_routing.py:103-105](../evals/run_routing.py)) should not change until Jev's agreement has been measured against hand labels. Transcripts kept with `--keep` from the routing runs supply a small labelled set (the last run scored 48 of 50, [evals/results/v3.md:43-55](../evals/results/v3.md)). Thirty to a hundred labelled examples is enough to set a first threshold.

Two caveats keep this honest. The behaviour evals fail any *Blueprint run under test* that uses the network beyond public-data hosts ([evals/README.md:125](../evals/README.md)). The grader therefore has to run in the harness process after the `claude -p` subprocess ends, never inside it. The steelman alternative also has to be tested. At 56 routing rows, Jev's cost saving over a Haiku-class judge is trivial. Its real value is a usable probability, since verbalised LLM confidence clusters at 80–100% ([Xiong et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063)). The harness should run Jev and a cheap Claude judge side by side and keep whichever agrees with the hand labels more often. If neither beats the regexes, drop the idea. That outcome is cheap to learn and costs the shipped skill nothing.

## Keep the kit integration opt-in, vendor-neutral and unable to approve on its own

The kit's coordinator is a more natural home for a typed check than the planner. It runs long, repeats the same judgement for every task, and already has machine-readable state in `tasks.json` (`acceptance[{how, expected}]`, `status`; [skill/templates/kit-tasks.json:1-45](../skill/templates/kit-tasks.json)). Claude Code exposes the right hooks for this. PreToolUse can deny or ask, with the most restrictive hook winning (deny > defer > ask > allow), and Stop or SubagentStop can block "done" ([Agent SDK hooks](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-sdk/hooks); [Blake Crosley](https://blakecrosley.com/blog/claude-code-hooks-explained), *snippet*). TypeSafe ships an official Claude Code skill for building with its API, but it offers no setting that turns Claude Code into a Jev-powered agent ([TypeSafe agent skill](https://docs.typesafe.ai/agent-skill), *snippet*).

Even so, this step should wait. Writing a Jev hook into Blueprint's kit templates would store a vendor choice in the skill, which `keeping-it-running` forbids: "Never take the options from a list stored in this skill" ([skill/references/keeping-it-running.md:15](../skill/references/keeping-it-running.md); [skill/references/route-operate.md:57](../skill/references/route-operate.md)). It would also cut against "Choose boring technology" ([skill/SKILL.md:118](../skill/SKILL.md)). Any later version should keep four properties. It is **vendor-neutral**: an optional "typed decision check" whose provider is researched at run time and dated. It is **ordered after deterministic checks**: tests, regexes on `expected`, and file existence run first, with the typed check used only for semantic criteria a regex cannot settle. It is **one-directional**: the check can send a worker's "done" back or escalate it to the coordinator's own run, but it can never set `done`, because "a worker's 'done' is a claim" and the coordinator "runs its checks itself" ([skill/templates/kit-01-project-instructions.md:36](../skill/templates/kit-01-project-instructions.md)). On PreToolUse it may only *add* an ask or a deny, while the stop list ("publishing or deploying, spending or signing up, deleting or overwriting …") stays a deterministic rule ([skill/templates/kit-01-project-instructions.md:61-70](../skill/templates/kit-01-project-instructions.md)). That ordering is what the Datadog result requires. It is **off by default and fail-open to the existing flow**. Enabling it means an AI-provider account in the person's name ([skill/references/non-coder-checklist.md:16](../skill/references/non-coder-checklist.md)), a key entered in the platform's secret store, a spending cap ([skill/references/keeping-it-running.md:12](../skill/references/keeping-it-running.md)), and a new "outside services that change" stream with an owner and a cadence ([skill/references/keeping-it-running.md:10-11](../skill/references/keeping-it-running.md)). Lightning mode can never assume it. If TypeSafe is unavailable or returns HTTP 429, the kit carries on exactly as it does today. Loop and stuck detection stays as the coordinator's existing deterministic counters ("failed twice", [skill/references/context-and-agents.md:42](../skill/references/context-and-agents.md)), which practitioners prefer to fuzzy classifiers ([MatrixTrak](https://matrixtrak.com/blog/agents-loop-forever-how-to-stop), *snippet*). Any wording added should go in an on-demand reference. Route New has only about 1,500 tokens of headroom under its 25,000-token budget (23,462 used, [CHANGELOG.md:8-10](../CHANGELOG.md); [scripts/route_budget.py:15-26](../scripts/route_budget.py)), and files read on demand are not counted ([scripts/route_budget.py:34-36](../scripts/route_budget.py)).

## Guide users' products to typed decisions in general, not to Jev by name

Blueprint currently treats AI inside a product only as a risk. Security baseline item 9 requires server-side calls, spend caps, least-privilege tools, confirmation before destructive actions, and untrusted input kept apart from instructions ([skill/references/software-design-detail.md:16-17](../skill/references/software-design-detail.md)). Checklist item 30 lists "Hijacked prompts, runaway cost, wrong answers" as a two-way door ([skill/references/non-coder-checklist.md:58](../skill/references/non-coder-checklist.md)). Blueprint has no guidance on when a product decision should be a typed classification rather than generated text. That gap is real, and filling it is cheap. One vendor-neutral line in item 30 or baseline item 9 would do it: *for yes/no, category or score decisions, prefer a typed classifier that returns a confidence; act automatically only above a threshold tested on the product's own examples, and send the rest to a person or a stronger model.* A matching prompt in the phase 4 Interfaces section would cover outputs and latency numbers ([skill/references/type-adaptations.md:19](../skill/references/type-adaptations.md)). That gives run-time research room to surface Jev, a cheap LLM with structured outputs, or a fine-tuned classifier.

When research does surface Jev, Blueprint's existing research rules already set the right standard. Every price and limit gets an "as of" date, and vendor benchmarks are labelled as vendor claims ([skill/references/04-research.md:7-14](../skill/references/04-research.md)). The strongest case against has to be heard as well. Raschka expects a special-purpose classifier to match or beat Jev on narrow, fixed-label tasks ([Raschka](https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/p/classifier-history-and-jev), *snippet*), and Goedecke notes that fast structured output already exists ([seangoedecke.com](https://www.seangoedecke.com/jev-means-structured-output-is-interesting-again/), *snippet*). On the evidence above, Jev fits **high-volume, latency-sensitive, low-to-medium-stakes** routing, triage, moderation and scoring where the options change too often to train a model, used as the first tier of a cascade. In one benchmark, putting Jev first matched frontier accuracy on spam with about 1 in 9 messages escalated, at 8.5x lower cost ([PavelRavvich](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PavelRavvich/jev-bench/main/article/jev-article.md)). Jev is a poor fit for security gates, legal or medical judgements, multi-label tasks, low volumes where a cheap LLM's cost is negligible, and regulated or EU-resident data, because it is US-only, offers zero data retention only on enterprise plans and reportedly signs no BAA. The product's AI-provider choice stays a two-way default under item 30 unless it touches personal data or lock-in, in which case it becomes a question for the person.

| Priority | Where | Action | Precondition to proceed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Do first | Eval harness | Stdlib Jev grader beside the regexes; disagreement or low confidence goes to human reading | Hand-labelled transcripts; head-to-head with a cheap Claude judge; response schema confirmed at docs.typesafe.ai |
| Do soon (cheap) | Product advice | One vendor-neutral line on typed decisions in checklist item 30 / baseline item 9 | Fits the token budget or goes in an on-demand file; no vendor named |
| Defer | Kits (coordinator) | Optional, off-by-default typed pre-check after deterministic checks; can only escalate | Harness results show value; TypeSafe SLA, stable limits and pricing out of early access |
| Avoid | Shipped planner | No Jev calls for route, size, doors, audit labels or Amend triage | None: blocked by Rules 6, 9, 10 and low call volume |
| Avoid | Anywhere | Jev as the only gate on irreversible or security actions; Jev inside `check_kit.py`/`check_tasks.py` | None |

## Conclusion

Jev's typed interface matches Blueprint's rubrics closely, and that match is easy to mistake for a reason to integrate. The useful question is where a cheap, fast, imperfectly calibrated judgement repeats often enough to pay off and can fail without harm. Inside Blueprint, the eval harness is the only place that meets both conditions today, and it also yields the labelled data needed to judge whether Jev deserves a role in the kits.

A second consequence matters too. Blueprint's own rules (dated research, no stored vendor lists, evidence before "done", ask before network use) already describe the safe way to use any decision model. The most durable contribution Jev can make is to prompt Blueprint to add a vendor-neutral principle that will outlast Jev's early-access pricing, signup policy and version alias: typed decisions with confidence thresholds and a human fallback.
